\item \textbf{Answer:} The aphorism 'the movement of progressive societies has hitherto been a movement from Status to Contract' is often misinterpreted as a deterministic prediction about societal evolution. In reality, this statement, attributed to legal scholar Roscoe Pound, reflects a descriptive analysis of the historical shift from rigid, status-based relationships to more flexible, contractual ones, emphasizing the role of individual autonomy and choice. This misunderstanding can obscure the ongoing complexities in constitutional law, where the balance between individual rights and societal obligations continues to evolve. By viewing the aphorism as a mere prediction, one risks overlooking the nuanced, contingent nature of legal development and the ongoing debates surrounding the nature of rights and contracts in a contemporary context. Thus, it is crucial to recognize this phrase as a commentary on historical trends rather than a formulaic forecast for the future.

\item \textbf{Answer:} The relationship between customary law and treaty provisions is significant, as treaties can play a crucial role in the formation, codification, and evolution of customary law. Firstly, treaties may codify existing customary law, providing a formal written agreement that encapsulates practices accepted as legally binding by states. Secondly, they can 'crystallise' customary law by solidifying emerging practices into recognized norms, thereby accelerating their acceptance. Lastly, treaties can lead to the emergence of new customary law by establishing new standards and behaviors that states adopt over time, contributing to the development of international legal norms. This interplay illustrates how treaties not only reflect existing customs but also actively shape and influence the evolution of customary law.
\\ \textit{Note:} correct because it emphasizes that treaties serve as formal codifications of existing customary law and mechanisms that can crystallize and accelerate the recognition of emerging norms, illustrating the dynamic interplay between these two sources of international law.
