\item \textbf{Answer:} The Doctrine of Worthier Title is a legal principle in property law that posits that a grantor cannot create a future interest in their heirs if the intention to do so is expressed in the grant. Instead, the doctrine holds that the heirs of the grantor will take by descent rather than through the grant, resulting in a reversion of property back to the grantor's estate. This affects the validity of a grantor's heirs because it prevents them from having a vested interest in the property, thereby preserving the grantor's ability to retain control over the property during their lifetime. As a result, any attempt to convey a fee simple subject to a future interest for the grantor's heirs is rendered ineffective, reinforcing the principle that the grantor's intent should not undermine established property rights.

\item \textbf{Answer:} Maine's aphorism that 'the movement of progressive societies has hitherto been a movement from Status to Contract' highlights a historical shift from rigid social hierarchies to more fluid contractual relationships among individuals. However, this notion is often misinterpreted as a deterministic prediction about the future trajectory of societal evolution, suggesting an inevitable progression toward an entirely contract-based society. Such a misinterpretation obscures the complexities of social change, as it implies a linear path that may not account for the persistent influence of status-based structures and the interplay of various social, economic, and political factors. Understanding this aphorism as a historical observation rather than a prescriptive forecast allows for a more nuanced analysis of contemporary societal dynamics and the potential for both regression and advancement within the realms of status and contract. This distinction is crucial for constitutional law, as it informs debates about individual rights, social equity, and the roles of governance in shaping societal relationships.
\\ \textit{Note:} The answer correctly identifies that Maine's aphorism describes a historical transition in societal organization from fixed statuses to flexible contracts, while also emphasizing that misinterpreting this concept as a deterministic prediction oversimplifies and overlooks the nuanced realities of social evolution.
