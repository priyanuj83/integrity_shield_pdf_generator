\item \textbf{Answer:} When unpolarized light encounters the first ideal linear polarizer, only half of the incident light intensity is transmitted, according to Malus's law, resulting in an intensity of I₁ = 0.5 I₀, where I₀ is the initial intensity of the unpolarized light. As this polarized light passes through the second polarizer, oriented at a 45-degree angle to the first, the transmitted intensity is given by I₂ = I₁ $cos^2($θ), where θ is the angle between the two polarizers. Substituting the values, we find I₂ = (0.5 I₀) $cos^2(45$^\ensuremath{\circ}) = (0.5 I₀)(0.5) = 0.25 I₀. Thus, the percentage of the original intensity that emerges after passing through both polarizers is 25\%. This demonstrates how the orientation of polarizers affects light transmission, leading to significant attenuation of the intensity.
\\ \textit{Note:} The answer correctly applies Malus's law to determine that the first polarizer transmits half of the unpolarized light intensity, and then it calculates the intensity transmitted through the second polarizer at a 45-degree angle by using the cosine squared of the angle, demonstrating the relationship between polarizer orientation and light intensity.
\item \textbf{Answer:} When a body is projected upward with an initial speed of 1000 cm/s, it will reach a maximum height of 510 cm before the effects of gravity cause it to decelerate to a stop and then descend. This maximum height can be calculated using the kinematic equation \( v^2 = u^2 + 2 as \), where \( v \) is the final velocity (0 cm/s at the peak), \( u \) is the initial velocity (1000 cm/s), \( a \) is the acceleration due to gravity (-980 cm/$s^2$, acting downwards), and \( s \) is the displacement (maximum height). Rearranging the equation to solve for \( s \) yields \( s = \frac{u^2}{2|a|} = \frac{(1000)^2}{2 \times 980} \), resulting in a maximum height of 510 cm. Factors influencing this height include the initial speed, the acceleration due to gravity, and air resistance, which can reduce the actual height achieved in real-world conditions.
\\ \textit{Note:} correct because it properly applies the kinematic equation to determine the maximum height reached by a body projected upward, taking into account the initial velocity, the deceleration due to gravity, and the relationship between initial velocity, final velocity, and displacement.
