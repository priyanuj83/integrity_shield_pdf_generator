\item \textbf{Answer:} To analyze the motion of a stone thrown horizontally from a bridge with an initial speed of 10 m/s, we first consider the vertical motion under the influence of gravity. The stone falls 80 m vertically, and using the equation of motion \( s = ut + \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \) (where \( s \) is the vertical distance, \( u \) is the initial vertical speed, \( g \) is the acceleration due to gravity, and \( t \) is time), we set \( u = 0 \) since the initial vertical velocity is zero. Rearranging the equation for time yields \( t = \sqrt{\frac{2 s}{g}} \). Substituting \( s = 80 \) m and \( g \approx 9.81 \) m/$s^2$, we find \( t \approx \sqrt{\frac{160}{9.81}} \approx 4 \) seconds. Thus, the stone takes approximately 4 seconds to reach the water, confirming the key answer.
\\ \textit{Note:} correct because it appropriately applies the kinematic equation for vertical motion, recognizing that the stone's initial vertical speed is zero, allowing for the calculation of the time to fall 80 m solely under the influence of gravity.
\item \textbf{Answer:} To analyze the motion of passengers on a carnival ride moving in a horizontal circle with a radius of 5.0 m, completing a revolution in 4.0 s, we first calculate the linear velocity (v) using the formula v = 2πr/T, where r is the radius and T is the period. Substituting the values gives v = 2π(5.0 m)/(4.0 s) = 3.93 m/s. The centripetal acceleration (a\_c) can then be calculated using the formula a\_c = $v^2$/r, leading to a\_c = (3.93 m/$s)^2$/(5.0 m) = 3.08 m/$s^2$. This acceleration indicates that a centripetal force must act upon the passengers, directed towards the center of the circular path, which is crucial for maintaining their circular motion. The implications of this acceleration in terms of safety suggest that the ride must be designed to ensure that the centripetal force is sufficient to keep passengers securely in their seats, preventing potential hazards associated with excessive acceleration or inadequate centripetal force.
\\ \textit{Note:} correct because it accurately applies the formulas for linear velocity and centripetal acceleration to determine the motion of passengers on a carnival ride, demonstrating the relationship between these calculations and the implications for centripetal force and safety.
